How can the non-stick performance of a coating be measured?

Part I · Fundamentals and the current state of non-stick surface technology

Guillermo Guerrero Vacas · Department of Mechanical Engineering · University of Córdoba

17 November 2026 · Part I of II · Narrative review · Leer en español

A water droplet sliding readily on a surface is a striking visual demonstration. But will food, a resin or polyurethane foam release just as easily? Answering this question requires distinguishing wetting from adhesion.

1. What do we mean by non-stick performance?

From an industrial perspective, non-stick performance is the ability of a surface to facilitate the separation of a material with which it has been in contact. It is not an intrinsic, universal property of a coating: it depends on both materials and their contact conditions.

Adhesion may arise from intermolecular interactions, chemical bonding and mechanical interlocking. Deformation and energy dissipation can also occur during separation. It is therefore important to distinguish the reversible work of adhesion from the mechanical energy of separation. [1]

Hydrophobicity (water repellency), oleophobicity (oil repellency), self-cleaning and resistance to fouling are related properties, but they are not equivalent.

2. Wetting, surface energy and mobility

The contact angle describes the geometry of a droplet on a solid. Under ideal equilibrium conditions on a smooth, chemically homogeneous surface, the Young–Dupré equation relates this angle to the reversible work of adhesion per unit area:

Wa = γLV (1 + cos θ)

γLV is the liquid surface tension and θ is the equilibrium contact angle. For a given liquid, a larger angle implies a lower thermodynamic work in this model; it does not give the release force of a cooked or cured material.

Moreover, two surfaces with similar static contact angles can display very different droplet mobility. Contact angle hysteresis — the difference between advancing and receding angles — reflects contact-line pinning. The tilt angle required to initiate sliding also depends on droplet volume and the test protocol. [4]

3. Surface texture matters too

The models developed by Wenzel (1936) and Cassie–Baxter (1944) describe two limiting wetting situations on rough surfaces: liquid penetration into the texture, or composite contact with solid and trapped air. [2] [3] The latter state may promote repellency but can collapse under pressure or contamination.

A texture that repels water may instead promote mechanical interlocking if a resin enters its irregularities before solidifying. There is no universal relationship between roughness and non-stick performance.

4. Main non-stick technologies

The state of the art includes established industrial solutions and emerging approaches. The following comparison is qualitative, not a universal performance ranking.

TechnologyUnderlying principleLimitations
Fluoropolymers (PTFE, PFA, FEP)Low surface energy and chemical stabilityWear and environmental challenges
Silicones and elastomersSurface chemistry and flexibilityMechanical and thermal resistance varies by formulation
Sol-gel ceramics and hybridsTailorable composition and structureRetention of functionality during service
Bioinspired surfacesMicro/nanoscale textures; re-entrant geometriesTexture damage, pressure and contamination
Liquid-infused surfaces (SLIPS)Liquid interface retained in the textureLubricant depletion and compatibility
Liquid-like surfacesFlexible molecular chains tethered to the solidScalability and durability

The design of superoleophobic surfaces with re-entrant geometries (Tuteja et al., 2007) and of Nepenthes-inspired liquid-infused surfaces (Wong et al., 2011) represented major developments. Recent reviews also describe smooth surfaces with molecular mobility that do not necessarily rely on a free liquid phase. [6] [7] [8] [9]

5. From laboratory to industrial applications

In food processing, composition, moisture and changes during cooking influence adhesion; Michalski and colleagues highlighted the diversity of adhesion mechanisms in foods. [5] In polymer demoulding, shrinkage, friction, geometry and mechanical properties must also be considered. Service conditions determine which technology is appropriate.

Two University of Córdoba studies published in 2024 and 2025 illustrate this point: for the formulations and cooking conditions investigated, fluoropolymer coatings showed advantages in effectiveness or durability over the ceramic coatings tested. These findings cannot be generalized to all formulations. [11] [12]

6. The challenge of fluorine-free alternatives

A 2026 review in Nature Reviews Chemistry examines fluorine-free strategies for repelling water and organic liquids through surface architectures and non-fluorinated chemistry. [10] Promising initial liquid repellency must be distinguished from performance after repeated use, cleaning or abrasion. Scalability, cost and life-cycle environmental impacts also matter.

7. Final reflection

The best non-stick surface is not necessarily the most hydrophobic or the smoothest: it is the one that facilitates material release under actual service conditions and retains its functionality over a suitable lifetime.
